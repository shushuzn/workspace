Detection of Spin- and Valley-Polarized States — New Methods Without Need for Strong Magnetic Fields

Opening
Have you heard of "valley" freedom? Not a valley of grain, but a hidden degree of freedom for electrons in two-dimensional materials.

In quantum materials, electrons carry charge and spin — but they can also occupy different "energy valleys." This is called "valley." What is it for? It is directly related to next-generation quantum computing.

But detecting valley polarization has always been a challenge: traditional methods require strong magnetic fields, creating extremely high barriers for experiments.

This paper solves the problem: no magnetic field needed, just two electrical wires to measure.

The Two Methods
How to measure?

Method one: thermoelectric effect.

When a temperature gradient is applied across the material, electrons flow and generate a voltage. The magnitude of this thermoelectric voltage signal is directly proportional to the valley polarization intensity — strong signal means strong valley polarization.

Method two: current rectification.

When electrical current flows through the material and we measure the current-voltage relationship, it shows obvious asymmetric rectification behavior. The magnitude of this rectification effect also has a one-to-one correspondence with the degree of valley polarization.

In other words: by simply measuring the temperature difference, or observing the asymmetry in current flow, we can determine how strong the valley polarization is in this material.

The fundamental reason: the spin polarization of Ising superconductors interacts with TMDC valley-polarized materials through a quantum effect called Andreev reflection. The electrical signal thereby reads out the invisible valley polarization.

The Junction Structure
The research studies a hybrid structure: one layer is an Ising superconductor with strong spin-orbit coupling, the other layer is a valley-polarized two-dimensional material, such as few-layer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDC).

The key question: what is the relationship between these two degrees of freedom? Can we detect valley polarization through an indirect method?

When a temperature gradient is applied, the spin polarization of the Ising superconductor, through Andreev reflection at the interface, affects the electrical response of the valley-polarized material. The resulting thermoelectric voltage signal magnitude directly correlates with valley polarization intensity.

The more clever method is current rectification: when current is applied and the current-voltage relationship is measured, it exhibits obvious asymmetric rectification behavior. This rectification effect magnitude also has a one-to-one correspondence with valley polarization degree.

Advantages
Compared to traditional methods, the new methods have three advantages:

First, no strong magnetic field needed. An ordinary electrical measurement setup suffices.

Second, no complex optical equipment needed. Any experimental physicist can do it.

Third, the signal directly corresponds to valley polarization intensity — measurement results are clear and reliable.

This is why researchers call them two brand-new valley polarization "probes."

Conclusion
In summary: thermoelectric effect and current rectification — two seemingly simple electrical phenomena — reveal a corner of the valley degree of freedom in two-dimensional quantum materials.

Detecting valley polarization has never been this simple.
